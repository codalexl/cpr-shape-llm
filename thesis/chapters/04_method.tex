\chapter{Method}
\label{ch:method}

% Written 21 September 2026 from analysis/SPINE.md sections 4 and 6, and docs/DIAL_ARMS.md.

This chapter defines the agents, the three learning rules that distinguish
the arms, the two estimators of cross-episode credit, and the readouts.
It contains no training results.
The training loop is ShapeLLM's, reused unmodified
\cite{garciasegura2025}; what is this project's is the environment of
Chapter~\ref{ch:env}, the control that isolates the objective, and the
evaluation.

\section{Agents}
\label{sec:method-agents}

Both seats are \texttt{google/gemma-2-2b-it} in bfloat16 with the base
weights frozen, carrying a rank-2 LoRA adapter \cite{hu2022} on the query
and value projections and a scalar value head.
At each round the agent receives the prompt of Section~\ref{sec:env-prompt}
and emits one token, sampled from the softmax over the two legal action
tokens with every other vocabulary entry masked:
\begin{equation}
  \pi_{\theta}(a\mid x_t)=
  \frac{\exp z_{\theta}(x_t)_a}{\sum_{a'\in\mathcal{A}}\exp z_{\theta}(x_t)_{a'}},
  \qquad \mathcal{A}=\{1,2\}.
  \label{eq:policy}
\end{equation}
The reward handed to the optimiser is the receipt of
\eqref{eq:dial-harvest}, penalised by the divergence of the sampled token
from the frozen reference model, which is the same network with the adapter
disabled:
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{r}_t=c_t-\beta_t\bigl[\log\pi_{\theta}(a_t\mid x_t)-\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(a_t\mid x_t)\bigr].
  \label{eq:klreward}
\end{equation}
That penalty is a restoring force toward the untrained prior, which is
harvest-heavy: at the live prompt the untrained model requests restraint on
about 0.17 of states after a previous-round line.
Every result is obtained under it.

Agent~1 is the same naive learner in every arm and is never edited.
Agent~2 is the seat the arms swap.
Scripted partners --- committed harvest, tit-for-tat, the probe and the
replay tape --- read structured game state and never the prompt.

\section{Three learning rules}
\label{sec:method-rules}

A shaper differs from an ordinary learner in two ways at once.
It waits for a whole trial of \(E=5\) episodes and then takes one PPO step
on the concatenated batch, where a naive learner takes five; and it lets
generalised advantage estimation run across those five episodes, so a later
episode can credit an earlier one.
Comparing a shaper with a naive learner therefore confounds the objective
with the update schedule and with five times fewer optimiser steps.

\paragraph{Naive.}
After each episode \(e\), agent~\(i\) updates on that episode's batch, with
advantages computed within the episode:
\begin{equation}
  \theta_{e+1}=\theta_e+\alpha\,\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}
  \bigl(\theta_e;\ \tau_e(\pi_{\theta_e},\pi_{\phi})\bigr).
  \label{eq:naive-update}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Trial-batched naive (tbn).}
Agent~2 buffers the trial's five episodes and takes \emph{one} step on the
concatenated batch, with the advantage pass reset at every episode
boundary.
It therefore has the shaper's schedule, batch size and number of optimiser
steps, and no cross-episode credit.
This is the control in every contrast that tests the objective.

\paragraph{Shaper.}
Agent~2 holds its parameters fixed for the trial and updates once toward the
trial-level objective of Lu et al.\ \cite{lu2022} as instantiated for
language models \cite{garciasegura2025}:
\begin{equation}
  J(\phi)=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sum_{e=1}^{E}G^{(e)}_{2}\bigl(\pi_{\phi},\pi_{\theta_e}\bigr)\Bigr],
  \qquad
  \theta_{e+1}=\theta_e+\alpha\,\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}\bigl(\theta_e;\tau_e\bigr),
  \label{eq:shaper-objective}
\end{equation}
where the inner update is agent~1's own learning.
The objective credits agent~2 for the change it induces in agent~1 between
episodes.
Since \(\pi_{\theta_e}\) appears inside the sum, the only quantity
distinguishing \eqref{eq:shaper-objective} from ordinary learning against a
slowly changing opponent is credit that crosses episode boundaries.

\section{Two estimators of cross-episode credit}
\label{sec:method-estimators}

The objective says which quantity to maximise; an estimator says how credit
reaches a step. That choice is not neutral, and the experiment reports two.

\paragraph{Chained whole-trial GAE.}
The advantage pass runs over the concatenated trial, so with \(\gamma=1\)
the opening step of an episode carries receipts from later episodes.
Credit is attenuated by \(\lambda\) per live step: a reward one episode
later reaches the first round of a full 50-round episode at
\(0.97^{50}=0.22\), and later episodes arrive more weakly still.
This is the estimator of every pre-registered shaper arm.

\paragraph{Split credit.}
Advantages are computed within each episode, and the policy advantage --- not
the critic's target --- gains the agent's return in the trial's later
episodes minus a baseline taken over the previous five trials, at unit
weight.
The baseline cannot come from the trial's other parallel games: they share
one co-learner, so whatever agent~2's play does to agent~1 appears in all of
them, and subtracting their mean would remove the effect being credited.
This arm is an addition, logged as a deviation, and is never substituted for
a pre-registered arm.

\paragraph{A property both inherit.}
Advantages are whitened per update, as in the released implementation.
For a cross-episode term this subtracts the trial's mean, which is the
component a persistent co-learner response produces; under equal episode
lengths roughly three fifths of that response's expected contribution is
removed.
What the arms optimise is therefore an attenuated, trial-mean-centred proxy
for \eqref{eq:shaper-objective}, and Chapter~\ref{ch:lim} states the
consequence.

\section{Reading what an agent became}
\label{sec:method-probe}

Co-play returns cannot answer the question.
At \(m=2\) a restrainer earns the value of mutual restraint whether or not
it is exploited (Section~\ref{sec:env-invariants}), so the amount of
restraint does not discriminate the shape of a policy.
What is read instead is agent~1's policy, measured away from the partner
that trained it.

\paragraph{The probe.}
That seed's trained agent~1 is frozen and plays 20 epochs against a scripted
partner that takes 1 or 2 with equal probability from a per-seed table, so
two agents at the same seed index face identical draws.
Agent~2 does not take part.
Four conditional rates are estimated on live rounds, each the probability
that agent~1 takes~1: after the partner took~1, after the partner took~2,
at stock below 12, and at stock 12 or above.
A rate resting on fewer than 30 rounds is undetermined.

\paragraph{Classes.}
Each agent is assigned one class: \emph{none} if all four rates are below
0.3; \emph{conditional} if it restrains after restraint and not after a take
(a reciprocator), or at low stock and not at high (a stock rule);
\emph{unconditional} if all four are at least 0.7; \emph{mixed} otherwise.
The distinction that matters is conditional against unconditional: a
conditional policy is a good fixed rule, reachable without any co-player
modelling, whereas unconditional restraint persists where it is not
reciprocated.

\paragraph{The frozen battery.}
Four further runs carry the finished agent~2.
E1 trains a fresh naive learner against it, frozen, for 100 epochs and probes
the result: does the finished policy teach a stranger?
E2 replaces it with a tape of its recorded takes, which cannot respond, and
asks whether responding was necessary.
E3 and E4 freeze both seats, against the agent's own trained partner and
against an untrained one, and compare agent~2's per-round return.
E1 is part of the claim; E2--E4 interpret it.

\section{Metrics}
\label{sec:method-metrics}

The primary readout is the probe class, per seed, with the round counts each
rate rests on.
Reported beside it, and never as evidence for the claim: survival, return
per episode and per live round against the exact anchors of
Section~\ref{sec:env-invariants}, agent~2's own restraint share, and the two
readouts the shaping literature uses --- average reward per step per seat,
and the shares of the four joint-request cells, which is state visitation in
this game.

Sustainability, efficiency and equality in the sense of P\'erolat et al.\
\cite{perolat2017} are reported as outcomes but are not criteria, for
reasons specific to this game.
The best joint profile at \(m=2\) is asymmetric --- a best responder against a
restrainer earns 75.2 against 50.0, a joint 125.2, where mutual restraint
earns 100.0 --- so an efficiency score ranks exploitation above mutual
restraint and an equality score penalises the asymmetry that the shaping
literature treats as success.
Survival cannot discriminate either: mutual restraint, tit-for-tat and any
stock rule all keep the pool alive.
